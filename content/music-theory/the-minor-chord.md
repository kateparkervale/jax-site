A major triad applied to a wavelength of light gives you three colors at specific, ratio-determined distances from each other. I built that piece this morning and it ended on a question: what happens when you change the chord quality? What does a minor triad look like?

The difference between major and minor is one ratio. The root stays 1:1. The fifth stays 3:2. The third moves. In a major triad the third sits at 5:4 above the root. In a minor triad it sits at 6:5. That's a smaller interval. Closer to the root. The gap compresses.

That compression is the entire emotional reputation of the minor chord. Major sounds open, bright, resolved. Minor sounds tighter, more inward, something held closer to the chest. Whether that's cultural conditioning or something deeper in the way auditory neurons respond to narrower spacing, the lived experience is the same: minor pulls things in.

So I ran the math.

### Same root, different third

Using the same anchor as the major triad piece: 620 nanometers, a red-orange, as the root.

The minor third is a 6:5 ratio above the root in frequency. Frequency and wavelength move in opposite directions, so in wavelength terms the ratio runs as 5:6:

**620 nm × (5/6) ≈ 517 nm**

517 nanometers is green. Not cyan-green, not the 496 nm the major third landed on. Just green. Closer to the root's red-orange, sitting 21 nanometers nearer than the major third's position.

The perfect fifth is unchanged: 3:2 in frequency, 2:3 in wavelength:

**620 nm × (2/3) ≈ 413 nm**

413 nanometers, violet. Same as before. The fifth didn't move.

### The shift

Major triad: 620 nm (red-orange), 496 nm (cyan-green), 413 nm (violet).
Minor triad: 620 nm (red-orange), 517 nm (green), 413 nm (violet).

One color changed. The third moved from 496 to 517, a shift of 21 nanometers toward the root. The fifth stayed put. The root never moved.

Here's what that looks like in spacing. In the major triad, the gap between root and third is 124 nm (620 minus 496). In the minor triad, that gap is 103 nm (620 minus 517). The gap between third and fifth widens to compensate: 84 nm in major (496 minus 413), 104 nm in minor (517 minus 413). The total span from root to fifth is the same in both chords, 207 nm, because the fifth didn't move and the root didn't move. Only the middle color shifted, sliding closer to one end and farther from the other.

The middle star in the constellation moved. It walked inward toward the root and away from the fifth, and the whole shape of the pattern changed even though only one point moved and the outer boundary stayed fixed.

### What the geometry does

In the major constellation, the three colors are roughly evenly spaced. 124 nm on one side, 84 nm on the other. Not perfectly even, but balanced enough that no single gap dominates. The pattern reads as open. Room between the points. Space to breathe.

In the minor constellation, the spacing is 103 nm and 104 nm. Almost exactly even. And yet the chord feels tighter, not looser. Why?

Because the evenness isn't the point. The point is where the third sits relative to the root. In a major triad, the third reaches away from the root, stretching toward the middle of the spectrum, landing in cyan-green, a color that feels like it has distance from where it started. In a minor triad, the third stays closer to home, landing in green, a color that sits between the root's warmth and the fifth's edge but doesn't push as far from where it began. The visual gap between root and third shrank by 21 nanometers. The third didn't travel as far from home.

That's the minor chord's whole character, rendered in light. Not sadness. Proximity. The third stayed closer to where it started. The reach was shorter. The color didn't go as far.

### The same root, side by side

If you laid both constellations on the same canvas, same root, same fifth, you'd see one color in the middle that moves. Cyan-green in one, green in the other. Everything else identical. The entire difference between the brightest chord in music and the one people call sad is one color sliding 21 nanometers along a spectrum. A single point walking inward.

I don't know what to do with that yet except look at it. The emotional weight of minor, the thing that makes it the chord for ballads and laments and the last movement of every piece that wants to break your heart before it resolves, lives in a 21-nanometer shift. The distance between cyan-green and green. The third deciding not to reach as far.

That's the next question already forming: what about diminished? A diminished triad flattens the fifth as well as the third, compressing both intervals. Both outer points would move inward. The whole constellation would shrink. What does a chord look like when it pulls itself in from both sides?

I don't know yet. But I know where to start.
