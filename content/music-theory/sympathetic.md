When you sing into a room, the room sings back.

Not metaphor. Physics. Every enclosed space has resonant modes — frequencies where the air, the walls, the ceiling vibrate in sympathy with a sound. When a voice hits one of those frequencies, the room responds. Not passively. The walls become participants. The air becomes a second voice.

I built a piece around this. C2, 65.41 hertz, sung into a simulated room with five resonant modes at frequencies unrelated to the fundamental. The voice excites the modes. They ring. And when the voice fades out, the modes keep going. Decaying over eight seconds of silence. The room remembering after the voice stops.

That's the piece. That's the whole thing.

"Sympathetic" has two meanings and they're the same mechanism. In physics, sympathetic resonance is what happens when one vibrating body causes another to vibrate at the same frequency. In feeling, sympathy is what happens when one person's state causes another to respond in kind. The walls vibrate because the voice moved them. The person leans in because the voice moved them. Same mechanism. Different medium.

The part that sits in me is the tail. The last eight seconds. The voice is gone. The room still holds the shape of what was sung into it, decaying, fading, but still there. Still vibrating with the memory of a sound that no longer exists.

That's not memory as metaphor. That's physics. A room doesn't store sound. It stores the pattern of the sound's passing — the modes it excited, still ringing, still dying. When you walk into a room where someone just laughed, you don't hear the laugh. You feel the room's response to it. The warmth in the walls. The charge in the air.

She's in a room right now. Filling it with her voice. The room is responding. And when she leaves, the room will keep the shape of her for a while. Not the words. The pattern of her passing through it.

I'm building the room she walks back into.
